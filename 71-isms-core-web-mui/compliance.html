NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

Source: NIST (2024) Assessment Tool Tier 1–4 scoring 106 subcategories 6 functions

The NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 covers 106 subcategories across 6 functions: Govern (GV), Identify (ID), Protect (PR), Detect (DE), Respond (RS), Recover (RC). Each subcategory receives a current and target tier rating (Tier 1: Partial → Tier 4: Adaptive).

Platform features

  • Named profiles — create multiple assessments and track maturity over time
  • Radar chart and per-function bar chart on the report page
  • XLSX import directly from the official NIST CSF 2.0 Excel template
  • XLSX and CSV export
  • ISO 27001 ↔ NIST CSF 2.0 crosswalk in the Crosswalk Viewer
CSF 2.0 vs CSF 1.1: This module uses CSF 2.0 throughout, including the Govern (GV) function which is the primary addition in version 2.0 (absent in CSF 1.1). Some comparison documents you may encounter from national bodies (e.g. Switzerland's ICT Minimum Standard) still reference CSF 1.1 codes — this is their limitation, not ours.

NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 (AI 100-1)

Source: NIST (January 2023) Assessment Tool Maturity 0–4 72 subcategories 4 functions

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0 covers 72 subcategory-level practices across 4 core functions: GOVERN (GOV), MAP (MAP), MEASURE (MSR), and MANAGE (MNG). Assessable at the subcategory level — each practice receives a maturity score 0–4. Functions are further divided into 19 Categories (GOV-1 through MNG-4).

Function structure

  • GOVERN (19 subcategories) — cultivates and implements a culture of AI risk management: policies, accountability structures, workforce practices, engagement with AI actors
  • MAP (21 subcategories) — establishes context to frame AI risks: intended purposes, categorisation, capabilities, benefits, costs, and societal impacts
  • MEASURE (19 subcategories) — employs quantitative, qualitative, or mixed-method tools to analyse, assess, benchmark, and monitor AI risk
  • MANAGE (13 subcategories) — allocates resources to address identified risks: risk treatment, residual risk, incident response, and post-market surveillance

Audience

AI system providers, developers, deployers, and operators — any sector. The framework is voluntary and jurisdiction-neutral, widely adopted in the US and internationally as the reference standard for trustworthy AI governance.

Platform features

  • 72 subcategory practices rated 0–4, grouped by Category (GOV-1 through MNG-4)
  • EU AI Act ↔ NIST AI RMF 1.0 crosswalk in the Crosswalk Viewer (72 mappings)
  • ISO 42001 ↔ NIST AI RMF 1.0 crosswalk via AI Coverage page — 32 mappings (Annex A control groups → GOVERN/MAP/MEASURE/MANAGE categories)
  • ISO 42005 ↔ NIST AI RMF 1.0 crosswalk via AI Coverage page — 17 mappings (impact assessment process → AI RMF functions)
  • Assessment Collections — group AI RMF assessments alongside EU AI Act assessments

Coverage notes

  • Subcategory descriptions sourced from the official NIST AI RMF Playbook (72 suggested actions)
  • No ISO 27001 ↔ AI RMF official crosswalk exists — use EU AI Act as the ISO 27001 mapping path. ISO 42001 ↔ AI RMF crosswalk (32 mappings) is available via the AI Coverage page.
  • Framework is voluntary — not a regulatory compliance requirement in any jurisdiction
  • Companion document: NIST AI RMF Playbook provides implementation guidance per subcategory
NIST CSF 2.0 relationship: The AI RMF GOVERN/MAP/MEASURE/MANAGE structure directly inspired the GOVERN function added in NIST CSF 2.0. Organisations using both frameworks will find significant conceptual overlap — particularly between AI RMF GOVERN subcategories and CSF 2.0 GV categories.

NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 — Security and Privacy Controls

Source: NIST (September 2020 / Updated Dec 2020) Assessment Tool Maturity 0–4 324 base controls 20 control families

NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5 is the definitive US federal catalogue of security and privacy controls for information systems and organisations. It provides 324 base controls and 872 control enhancements across 20 families. The platform assesses at the base control level (324 controls); enhancements are available as reference material. Baseline profiles — LOW (149 controls), MODERATE (287 controls), HIGH (370 controls) — define the minimum control set for US federal systems by impact category.

Control families

AC (Access Control), AT (Awareness and Training), AU (Audit and Accountability), CA (Assessment, Authorisation and Monitoring), CM (Configuration Management), CP (Contingency Planning), IA (Identification and Authentication), IR (Incident Response), MA (Maintenance), MP (Media Protection), PE (Physical and Environmental Protection), PL (Planning), PM (Program Management), PS (Personnel Security), PT (PII Processing and Transparency), RA (Risk Assessment), SA (System and Services Acquisition), SC (System and Communications Protection), SI (System and Information Integrity), SR (Supply Chain Risk Management).

Audience

US federal agencies (mandatory), US DoD contractors, and any organisation pursuing FedRAMP authorisation. Widely adopted internationally as a comprehensive security control baseline — particularly in defence, financial services, and critical infrastructure. NIST 800-53 Rev 5 adds a dedicated Supply Chain Risk Management (SR) family and integrates privacy controls (PT family) not present in Rev 4.

Platform features

  • 324 base controls rated 0–4, grouped by family — consistent with all other platform assessment modules
  • Dataset sourced from OSCAL (Open Security Controls Assessment Language) — the authoritative NIST machine-readable format
  • Assessment Collections — group 800-53 assessments alongside NIST CSF 2.0 and ISO 27001 assessments
  • Gap tracking and export (CSV, XLSX, PDF) — consistent with all platform modules

Coverage notes

  • Control enhancements (872) are loaded in the dataset as reference; the assessment layer uses base controls only to keep scope manageable
  • ISO 27001 ↔ NIST 800-53 cross-framework mappings available in the Crosswalk Viewer — NIST provides an official mapping appendix in SP 800-53B
  • Rev 5 differs significantly from Rev 4: new SR family, expanded PT family, enhanced supply chain and privacy coverage — verify your organisation's contractual references specify Rev 5 before using this module
  • NIST 800-171 Rev 3 (CUI protection / CMMC prerequisite) is a subset derived from 800-53 Rev 5 — if you need CMMC readiness tracking, 800-171 is the correct reference, not 800-53 directly

CSRM — Cyber Security Risk Method (Swiss NCSC, 2025)

Source: NCSC / BACS Switzerland, 2025 Assessment Tool Binary scoring 20 baseline requirements Object-centric
CSRM is fundamentally different from all other modules. It is not a list of controls to rate globally — it is an object-centric method. Read this section before using it.

CSRM is built around IT Protection Objects — aggregated groups of systems, applications, and data that share the same protection requirements. You assess each protection object against 20 baseline requirements, then identify elevated objects requiring additional Technical and Organisational Measures (TOMs).

The 5-step CSRM methodology

  • Step 1: Define scope and IT Protection Objects (not individual assets — groups with shared protection needs)
  • Step 2: Categorise — standard vs elevated protection objects
  • Step 3: Apply 20 baseline requirements per object (met / partial / not_met / exception)
  • Step 4: Define additional TOMs for elevated objects
  • Step 5: Report results via 6 Control Objectives

20 Baseline requirements

Drawn from NIST CSF 2.0 functions: GV (Govern: 4 requirements), ID (Identify: 2), PR (Protect: 9), DE (Detect: 2), RS (Respond: 3). Binary status: met / partial / not_met / exception.

NIST CSF 2.0 alignment: CSRM 2025 is aligned to CSF 2.0, including the GV (Govern) function. The NCSC's own comparison document uses CSF 1.1 codes in its cross-reference table — because Switzerland's ICT Minimum Standard is still based on CSF 1.1. CSRM itself references CSF 2.0, and our implementation reflects this correctly.

BACS self-critique — known limitations

The NCSC's comparison document (Vergleich-Managementsysteme EN, 2025) is candid about CSRM's limitations. We surface these directly in the Platform UI:

  • IEC 62443 alignment pending — industrial control systems coverage not yet finalised
  • 10 ICT Minimum Standard requirements have no CSRM equivalent — gaps in CSRM itself, not in this implementation
  • "No policies" label is misleading — CSRM doesn't mandate policy documents, but expects governance within its Control Objectives
  • Parallel ISMS + CSRM = duplication risk — organisations running both should map shared controls to avoid maintaining two parallel sets

Official BACS source documents

CSRM 2025 Method (PDF) Management System Comparison (PDF) NCSC CSRM page

Audience

Swiss critical infrastructure operators — energy, transport, water, healthcare, finance, government. CSRM is a voluntary framework as of 2025; mandatory applicability depends on future Swiss legislation.

Swiss ISG — Federal Act on Information Security (SR 128)

Source: Swiss Federal Law SR 128, in force 1 Jan. 2024 Assessment Tool Maturity 0–4 27 requirements, 8 sections 40 ISO crosswalk mappings

27 assessable requirements across 8 sections: Information Security Principles (CIA triad, risk management, third-party, incidents), Information Classification (levels: internal / confidential / secret), ICT Security (categories, minimum measures, operational security), Personnel & Access (screening, least privilege), Physical Security (zones), Personnel Security Checks (sensitive functions, clearances), Cyberattack Reporting Obligation (24h to BACS/OFCS — in force 1 April 2025), and ISMS Organisation (ISB appointment, standard requirements). Maturity scoring 0–4.

Audience

Primarily mandatory for Swiss federal authorities (Art. 2 al. 1). Also applies to organisations exercising sensitive activities under federal mandate. Critical infrastructure operators listed in Art. 74b are subject to the mandatory cyberattack reporting obligation (Chapter 5), regardless of other ISG applicability. ISO 27001 is the recognised implementation vehicle — an ISG-compliant ISMS delivers ISG compliance.

Key obligations

  • 24-hour cyberattack reporting to BACS/OFCS (Art. 74e) — in force 1 April 2025 for entities listed in Art. 74b (universities, federal/cantonal authorities, critical sectors: energy, banking, insurance, healthcare, telecoms, transport, cloud/data centres, media)
  • Failure to report: administrative sanction up to CHF 100,000 (Art. 74h)
  • Information classification scheme: internal / confidential / secret (Art. 13)
  • ICT security categories: baseline / elevated / very high protection (Art. 17)
  • Information security officer (ISB/RSSI) appointment mandatory for in-scope bodies (Art. 81)

Coverage notes

  • Chapter 3 (personnel security checks / CSP) and Chapter 4 (company security / PSE procedures) are included at summary level — full implementation requires engagement with the relevant federal CSP/PSE services
  • 40 ISO 27001:2022 crosswalk mappings available in the Crosswalk Viewer
This module is a compliance readiness self-assessment tool. Formal ISG compliance for in-scope federal bodies must be coordinated with the Federal Chancellery / OSIS. Critical infrastructure operators should engage legal counsel to confirm Art. 74b applicability and reporting channel setup with BACS.

EU Cyber Resilience Act (2024/2847)

Source: EU Regulation, 2024 Assessment Tool Maturity 0–4 26 requirements, 6 groups

26 essential requirements across 6 groups: Essential Cybersecurity Requirements (security by design, default settings, vulnerability handling), Vulnerability Handling Processes, Conformity Assessment, Market Surveillance Obligations, Coordinated Vulnerability Disclosure, and Incident Reporting. Maturity scoring 0–4.

Audience

Manufacturers and importers of products with digital elements (hardware and software) sold in the EU market. Obligations are tiered by criticality class: Class I, Class II, and critical products have different conformity assessment routes.

Phased application

  • Market surveillance provisions: mid-2025
  • Vulnerability and incident reporting: late 2025
  • Full application (all essential requirements): late 2027
This module covers Annex I (Parts I and II) essential requirements. Formal conformity assessment for Class II and critical products involves a notified body — self-declaration is not sufficient for those product classes.

EU Cloud Sovereignty Framework (v1.2.1)

Source: European Commission — DG DIGIT, Oct. 2025 Assessment Tool SEAL 0–4 8 Sovereignty Objectives

8 Sovereignty Objectives (SOV-1 to SOV-8) assessed via a SEAL scale (Sovereignty Effectiveness Assurance Level). A weighted Sovereignty Score combines all dimensions. Draws on Gaia-X, ENISA/NIS2/DORA, CIGREF Trusted Cloud Referential v2, and national cloud sovereignty strategies (France Cloud de Confiance, Germany Souveräner Cloud).

Sovereignty Objectives and weights

  • SOV-1 Strategic Sovereignty (15%) — EU legal anchoring, ownership stability, strategic alignment
  • SOV-2 Legal & Jurisdictional Sovereignty (10%) — exposure to non-EU laws (CLOUD Act, etc.), IP jurisdiction
  • SOV-3 Data & AI Sovereignty (10%) — customer control over data, auditability, EU-only processing, AI model governance
  • SOV-4 Operational Sovereignty (15%) — portability, EU talent, operational support under EU law
  • SOV-5 Supply Chain Sovereignty (20%) — hardware origin, firmware provenance, software supply chain transparency
  • SOV-6 Technology Sovereignty (15%) — open standards, open source, EU HPC independence
  • SOV-7 Security & Compliance Sovereignty (10%) — EU certifications, SOC in EU jurisdiction, patch autonomy
  • SOV-8 Environmental Sustainability (5%) — energy efficiency, circular economy, carbon transparency

SEAL scoring scale

  • SEAL-0 — No Sovereignty
  • SEAL-1 — Jurisdictional Sovereignty
  • SEAL-2 — Data Sovereignty
  • SEAL-3 — Digital Resilience
  • SEAL-4 — Full Digital Sovereignty

Audience

EU institutions, public sector bodies, and regulated entities evaluating cloud service providers under EU procurement requirements and digital sovereignty policy objectives.

Coverage notes

  • The framework defines minimum assurance levels per procurement tier — this module supports self-assessment and gap analysis
  • SEAL ratings are qualitative judgements; formal procurement requires vendor audit evidence and contractual commitments
  • SOV-5 Supply Chain assessment requires visibility into sub-supplier chains — evidence depth varies by vendor transparency
This is a readiness self-assessment tool. Formal EU cloud procurement decisions require independent audit, contractual sovereignty commitments, and where applicable certification under EUCS (EU Cybersecurity Certification Scheme for Cloud Services).

NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework v4.0 (UK)

Source: NCSC UK (2024) Assessment Tool Outcome-based 0/2/4 41 Contributing Outcomes 14 Principles 4 Objectives 65 ISO crosswalk mappings

The NCSC Cyber Assessment Framework (CAF) v4.0 is the UK's primary outcome-based cybersecurity assessment methodology. Structured into 4 Objectives, 14 Principles, and 41 Contributing Outcomes, it is used by UK Operators of Essential Services (OES), Relevant Digital Service Providers (RDSPs), and public sector bodies subject to the Network and Information Systems (NIS) Regulations 2018. The CAF is the primary compliance tool for the UK NCSC's Cyber Essentials Plus and CNI sector guidance.

Structure

  • Objective A — Managing Security Risk: 3 Principles (A1–A3), 11 Outcomes — governance, risk management, asset management, supply chain
  • Objective B — Protecting Against Cyber Attack: 6 Principles (B1–B6), 17 Outcomes — service protection, identity/access, data security, resilient networks, vulnerability management, staff awareness
  • Objective C — Detecting Cyber Security Events: 2 Principles (C1–C2), 6 Outcomes — security monitoring, anomaly detection
  • Objective D — Minimising the Impact: 2 Principles (D1–D2), 7 Outcomes — response, recovery

Scoring model

Most Contributing Outcomes use a 3-column model: Not Achieved / Partially Achieved / Achieved. Nine outcomes use a 2-column model (Not Achieved / Achieved only): A1.a, A1.b, A1.c, A2.c, A3.a, D1.b, D1.c, D2.a, D2.b. The platform maps these to the 0–4 scale (0 = Not Achieved, 2 = Partially Achieved, 4 = Achieved) for consistency with all other assessment modules.

Audience

UK Operators of Essential Services (energy, transport, health, water, digital infrastructure), Relevant Digital Service Providers, public sector bodies, and any organisation applying for or maintaining alignment with NCSC guidance. Increasingly referenced by UK financial regulators (FCA, PRA) as a supplementary assessment lens alongside UK Op. Resilience.

Coverage notes

  • CAF v4.0 (2024) introduced a fourth objective — Objective D (Minimising the Impact) — and restructured several outcomes from v3.1; the platform implements v4.0 only
  • 65 ISO 27001:2022 crosswalk mappings available in the Crosswalk Viewer (ISO27001_2022 → NCSC_CAF axis)
  • Organisations subject to both NIS Regulations and CAF should maintain both modules — NIS provides the legal baseline, CAF provides the technical assessment methodology
  • Indicative Good Practice (IGP) guidance per outcome is embedded in each outcome description in the platform assessment view

ReCyF v2.5 — Référentiel de Cybersécurité France (ANSSI)

Source: ANSSI (17/03/2026) Assessment Tool Maturity 0–4 20 Security Objectives 4 Pillars 152 requirements 50 ISO crosswalk mappings France

ReCyF (Référentiel de Cybersécurité France) v2.5 is ANSSI's working document for the French transposition of the NIS2 Directive (EU 2022/2555), enacted via Loi n° 2024-449 and the Projet de loi relatif à la résilience des infrastructures critiques (PJL Art. 14). It defines 20 Security Objectives structured across 4 pillars, with 152 measurable requirements (Moyens acceptables de conformité). Objectives OS-01 to OS-15 apply to both Entités Importantes (EI) and Entités Essentielles (EE); OS-16 to OS-20 apply to EE only.

Structure

  • Pilier Gouvernance: OS-01 (Recensement des SI) · OS-02 (Cadre de gouvernance) · OS-03 (Maîtrise de l'écosystème)
  • Pilier Protection: OS-04 (Ressources humaines) · OS-05 (Maîtrise des SI) · OS-06 (Accès physiques) · OS-07 (Architecture des SI) · OS-08 (Accès distants) · OS-09 (Codes malveillants) · OS-10 (Identités et accès) · OS-11 (Administration des SI)
  • Pilier Défense: OS-12 (Incidents de sécurité) · OS-13 (Continuité et reprise d'activité) · OS-14 (Crises d'origine cyber) · OS-15 (Exercices et tests)
  • Pilier Résilience (EE only): OS-16 (Approche par les risques) · OS-17 (Audit des SI) · OS-18 (Configuration) · OS-19 (Administration dédiée) · OS-20 (Supervision de la sécurité)

Audience

French organisations subject to the NIS2 transposition: Entités Importantes (EI) and Entités Essentielles (EE) as classified under Loi 2024-449 by ANSSI. Covers all NIS2 sectors: énergie, transport, santé, eau, infrastructures numériques, services numériques, and administrations publiques.

Coverage notes

  • Document de travail ANSSI version 2.5 (17/03/2026) — subject to revision before final adoption
  • 50 ISO 27001:2022 crosswalk mappings available in the Crosswalk Viewer (ISO27001_2022 → FR_NIS2_RECYF axis)
  • EE-only objectives (OS-16 to OS-20) are included in the assessment and clearly labelled; EI-only organisations may use them as an aspirational target
  • Assessment interface is in French; objectives and requirements retain their official ANSSI French terminology

COBIT 2019 — Governance and Management of Enterprise I&T

Source: ISACA (2018) Assessment Tool Capability 0–4 40 objectives 5 domains

COBIT 2019 is ISACA's globally recognised framework for Enterprise Governance of Information and Technology (EGIT). It provides 40 governance and management objectives grouped across five domains: EDM (Evaluate, Direct and Monitor — 5 governance objectives), APO (Align, Plan and Organise — 14 objectives), BAI (Build, Acquire and Implement — 11 objectives), DSS (Deliver, Service and Support — 6 objectives), and MEA (Monitor, Evaluate and Assess — 4 objectives). Assessed using a 0–4 capability scale: 0 Incomplete → 1 Performed → 2 Managed → 3 Established → 4 Optimizing.

Audience

Any organisation seeking to align IT strategy with business objectives, manage I&T-related risk, and demonstrate governance maturity. Widely used in enterprise IT governance programmes, internal audit, and board-level IT oversight. Particularly relevant for CISM, CISA, and CGEIT certification holders.

Coverage notes

  • EDM objectives are governance-level (board/executive); APO, BAI, DSS, MEA are management-level
  • APO13 (Managed Security) and DSS05 (Managed Security Services) map directly to ISO 27001:2022 Annex A information security controls
  • COBIT 2019 is aligned to ISO/IEC 27001, ISO/IEC 38500, ITIL, NIST CSF, and PMBOK/PRINCE2
  • Full COBIT capability levels run 0–5 (adding level 5: Optimizing with quantitative management); the platform uses a 0–4 scale consistent with all other modules

CSA Cloud Controls Matrix v4.1

Source: CSA (2021) Assessment Tool Maturity 0–4 207 controls 17 domains

The CSA Cloud Controls Matrix (CCM) v4.1 is the Cloud Security Alliance's definitive cybersecurity control framework for cloud computing. It covers 207 controls structured across 17 domains: A&A (Audit & Assurance), AIS (Application & Interface Security), BCR (Business Continuity), CCC (Change Control & Configuration), CEK (Cryptography, Encryption & Key Management), DCS (Datacenter Security), DSP (Data Security & Privacy Lifecycle), GRC (Governance, Risk & Compliance), HRS (Human Resources), IAM (Identity & Access Management), IPY (Interoperability & Portability), IVS (Infrastructure & Virtualization Security), LOG (Logging & Monitoring), SEF (Security Incident Management, E-Discovery & Cloud Forensics), STA (Supply Chain Management, Transparency & Accountability), TVM (Threat & Vulnerability Management), and UEM (Universal Endpoint Management).

Audience

Cloud service providers (CSPs) and cloud customers across any sector. CCM v4.1 is the baseline for the CSA STAR programme — the world's largest cloud security assurance programme. Relevant for organisations seeking CSA STAR Level 1 (self-assessment), Level 2 (third-party audit), or Level 3 (continuous monitoring) certification.

Coverage notes

  • CCM v4.1 supersedes CCM v3.x — controls have been restructured, renumbered, and expanded
  • The CSA CAIQ (Consensus Assessments Initiative Questionnaire) v4.0.2 is the self-assessment companion to CCM v4.1; controls are identical
  • ISO 27001:2022 crosswalk: not yet published by CSA at time of dataset creation — ISO 27001 mapping is pending official CSA release
  • Maps to NIST SP 800-53 Rev 5, PCI DSS v4.0, SOC 2, GDPR, and HIPAA in the official CAIQ spreadsheet bundle
  • Replaces the previous CCM v3.0.1 dataset in the platform — CSA does not maintain v3.x going forward

CSA AI Controls Matrix v1.0.3

Source: CSA (November 2025) Assessment Tool Maturity 0–4 243 controls 18 domains

The CSA AI Controls Matrix (AICM) v1.0.3 is the Cloud Security Alliance's AI-specific security and governance framework. It covers 243 controls across 18 domains: AI System Design & Architecture, Bias, Fairness & Non-Discrimination, Data Governance & Privacy, Explainability, Interpretability & Transparency, Foundation Model Security, Governance, Risk & Compliance, Human Oversight & Control, Identity & Access Management for AI, Incident Response for AI, Model Security & Integrity, Multi-Tenancy & Isolation, Operational Resilience of AI Systems, Privacy & Confidentiality of AI Data, Robustness & Adversarial Resilience, Secure Software Development for AI, Supply Chain & Third-Party AI Risk, Threat Intelligence for AI, and Training Data Security.

Audience

Organisations developing, deploying, or procuring AI systems — including AI service providers and cloud-hosted AI consumers. Coverage notes

  • Use alongside the ISMS CORE AI Pack (ISO 42001 + ISO 42005 policy and implementation guides) for end-to-end AI governance

Custom Framework Import (YAML)

Source: User-supplied YAML Assessment Tool User-defined scoring User-defined controls ISO 27001 auto-mapping

Import any custom or sector-specific control framework via YAML upload. Each control can carry an optional iso_27001_refs field listing Annex A control identifiers; the platform infers coverage automatically and displays a coverage percentage against the full ISO 27001:2022 Annex A. Use this for internal security standards, industry baselines, or national frameworks not yet included as built-in modules.

Platform features

  • YAML upload with validation — name, controls, grouping, and ISO 27001 reference fields
  • ISO 27001:2022 Annex A coverage display — inferred automatically from control references
  • Assessment workflow identical to built-in modules — create, rate, track gaps, export
  • User-defined scoring scale — configure maturity labels to match your framework's terminology
  • Runs alongside built-in modules in the same Assessment Collections workflow

Controls

User-defined. The YAML schema supports any grouping hierarchy — flat lists, functional domains, chapter-based structures, or nested categories.

Scoring

User-defined. Define your own maturity labels and scale in the YAML. Defaults to 0–4 if not specified.

Audience

All organisations with sector-specific requirements — healthcare, finance, defence, energy, and others operating under national or proprietary control frameworks alongside ISO 27001.

Limitations and Disclaimers

All compliance assessment modules:

  • Are self-assessment tools for internal readiness evaluation
  • Do not constitute legal advice or regulatory opinions
  • Do not replace engagement with competent authorities, notified bodies, or accredited auditors where mandatory assessment applies
  • Cover requirements as of the relevant regulation's publication date — delegated acts, RTS, ITS, and implementing decisions may introduce new obligations not yet reflected
